2 · What's inside a computer? 10 min

Hardware + software

Hardware is the parts you can touch: chips, screen, keyboard, wires.
Software is the instructions that tell the hardware what to do: games, apps, Scratch, and soon your own Python programs.

Hardware without software is like a piano with nobody playing it. Software without hardware is like sheet music with no piano.

Source: Code.org & Khan Academy — How Computers Work: Hardware & Software

Input → Process → Output

Every computer does three things:

  1. Input: takes in information (keyboard, mouse, microphone, camera, buttons).
  2. Process: works on it (the chips do the maths).
  3. Output: gives a result (screen, speaker, printer, a beep).

Example: a microwave. You press "30 seconds" (input) → its chip counts down (process) → it heats and beeps (output). So a microwave has a tiny computer inside!

Source: Code.org — How Computers Work: CPU, Memory, Input & Output

Two kinds of brain: CPU and GPU

🧠 The CPU (Central Processing Unit) is like a few super-clever chefs. Each one can cook any recipe, step by step, very fast.

💪 The GPU (Graphics Processing Unit) is like thousands of helpers. Each one only does a simple job, but they all work at the same time.

Your screen is made of millions of tiny dots called pixels. A normal HD screen has 1920 × 1080 = 2,073,600 of them! The GPU's helpers colour lots of pixels at once, many times every second. That's why games and videos look smooth.

Source: NVIDIA — What's the difference between a CPU and a GPU?

Misconceptions to watch for:
(1) "The GPU is just better." No: each GPU helper is much simpler than a CPU chef. GPUs win only when a job splits into many identical, independent pieces. The CPU wins at step-by-step jobs where each step depends on the last.
(2) "Software is the stuff on the screen." The screen is output; software is the instructions behind it.
Link back to 1.1: both the CPU and the GPU are made of billions of transistors, the tiny switches. A switch is either on or off. Computers write that as 1 and 0. That's all he needs on binary for now. It comes back properly later.

3 · Unplugged: The Human GPU race 12 min

Round 1: Human CPU. You colour all 100 squares of Grid A by yourself. Your teacher times you.

Round 2: Human GPU. Grid B is split into 4 quarters. You and 3 helpers each colour one quarter at the same time. Time it!

Write both times. How many times faster was the team?

Race timer: press Lap when each round finishes.

Then: be a programmer. Write the software for making a jam sandwich: numbered instructions, one action per line. Then label your kitchen:

  • What is the hardware? (knife, bread, you…)
  • What is the input, the process and the output?
After the race, ask the key question: "What if the picture needed each square to be coloured based on the square before it?" Then the team can't split it, and one fast person (a CPU) is better. That's the whole CPU/GPU idea.
Keep the sandwich recipe to about 5 minutes. Don't run the "follow it literally" game. Save that for Lesson 2.1 (the Robot Parent game).

4 · Investigate: Spy on your own computer 8 min

  1. Press Ctrl + Shift + Esc to open Task Manager. (Or right-click the Start button and choose Task Manager.)
  2. Click the Performance tab.
  3. Click CPU. Find Cores: that's how many "clever chefs" your CPU has!
  4. Click GPU to see your graphics chip's name and how busy it is.

Source: Microsoft — How to check your GPU · Microsoft — GPUs in the Task Manager
