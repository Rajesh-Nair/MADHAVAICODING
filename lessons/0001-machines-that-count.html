Machines That Count

People have always needed to count: stars, sheep, money, the distance to the Moon. This is the story of how counting machines grew from beads on a stick to chips with billions of tiny parts.

⏱ 45 minutes✏️ Coder's Journal + pencil🫘 20–30 dried beans, coins or buttons📱 A calculator
Goal of the session: Madhav leaves able to put the big inventions in order and explain why computers got smaller (transistors → chips). No coding today. The win is the "human vs machine" race and the shock of 2,300 → 28 billion.
Prep: print the bean abacus sheet (button at the bottom), get beans, a calculator and his journal. Answers to the race sums and homework guidance are in teacher/0001-machines-that-count-key.md.
Tone: wonder first. Every machine was someone's clever idea. That's what he'll be doing too.

1 · Warm-up 5 min

Open your Coder's Journal to a fresh page. Title it "Mission Log 1: Machines That Count". Then, without looking anything up, write:

  1. What do you think a computer is? (One sentence. There's no wrong answer yet!)
  2. List 5 things in your house that can do maths or count.
Don't correct his definition. You'll come back to it at the end of Lesson 1.2. Count anything with a chip as "doing maths" (microwave timer, phone, TV remote). If he lists only obvious ones (laptop, phone), prompt: "What about the kitchen?"

2 · The story of counting machines 12 min

Read each stop together. Before you read a stop, guess what came next.

  1. 🧮 Long, long ago

    The abacus

    Beads on rods. Each rod is a place: ones, tens, hundreds. Slide beads to add. No electricity, just fingers and a clever idea: place value.

    Source: Computer History Museum — Revolution: from the abacus to the smartphone

  2. ⚙️ 1642

    The Pascaline

    A French mathematician, Blaise Pascal, built a box of toothed gears that could add. Turn one wheel all the way round, and it pushes the next wheel on by one. That's carrying, done by a machine!

    Source: CHM — Pascal Adder (Pascaline)

  3. 🏭 1820s–1840s

    Babbage's Difference Engine

    Charles Babbage designed a giant gear machine to calculate tables of numbers automatically. It was too hard to build in his lifetime. In 1991, a museum built it from his drawings, and it worked exactly as he planned.

    Source: CHM — The Babbage Engines

  4. 💡 1945

    ENIAC: the electronic giant

    No gears, just electricity. ENIAC used about 18,000 vacuum tubes (glass switches a bit like light bulbs), weighed 30 tons and filled a huge room. It was over 1,000 times faster than earlier machines. A tube burned out every day or two, and someone had to find which one!

    Source: CHM — ENIAC

  5. 🔌 1947

    The transistor

    Scientists at Bell Labs invented a transistor: a tiny switch with no glass and no glowing hot parts. It does the same job as a vacuum tube but is much smaller and much more reliable. They won the Nobel Prize for it.

    Sources: CHM — Inventing the Transistor · Bell Labs — 1956 Nobel Prize

  6. 🔲 1971

    The microprocessor

    Intel squeezed a whole computer "brain" onto one chip: the Intel 4004, with about 2,300 transistors. It fit on your fingertip.

    Source: Intel — The 4004

  7. 🚀 Today

    Chips with billions of switches

    A modern laptop chip like Apple's M4 has about 28 billion transistors, still about the size of a fingernail. The giant AI chips that train tools like ChatGPT and Claude have even more.

    Source: Apple — M4 chip

Likely misconceptions:
(1) "One person invented the computer." It was many people over centuries, each building on the last. Hammer this home: it's the story of the whole course.
(2) "Transistors are batteries or wires." No, they're switches: on or off. Flick a light switch to show it.
(3) ENIAC (1945) came before the transistor (1947). He may assume small came first.
Listen for: him connecting "smaller switches" → "smaller, faster computers".

Predict before you peek

ENIAC weighed 30 tons. Roughly how heavy is that?

3 · Unplugged: Human vs Machine race 12 min

Part A: Be an abacus. Use the bean abacus sheet (or draw 3 columns in your journal: Hundreds · Tens · Ones).

  • Put beans in each column to show a number. 2 beans in Tens + 3 in Ones = 23.
  • To add, put in more beans. Rule: if a column reaches 10 beans, take all 10 out and put 1 bean in the column to the left. That's carrying, just like the Pascaline's gears!

Part B: The race. Your teacher times you doing each sum with beans. Then do the same sums on a calculator. Write both times in your journal.

  1. 34 + 25
  2. 47 + 38
  3. 126 + 275
  4. 389 + 456
  5. 999 + 1 🤯

Race timer: press Lap after each sum.

Do the bean round first, then the calculator round. Let him feel how slow the carrying is in sum 5 (three carries in a row!). That's exactly the problem Pascal's gears solved.
Ask: "The calculator is faster. But what's it actually doing inside?" Answer: carrying, with switches instead of beans. Don't explain binary yet; that's 1.2.

4 · Investigate: How big is the jump? 6 min

The 1971 chip had 2,300 transistors.
A 2024 laptop chip has about 28,000,000,000.

Use the calculator: 28,000,000,000 ÷ 2,300 = ?

If the 1971 chip were one grain of rice, today's chip would be a pile of rice bigger than you'd ever want to count by hand!

Write the answer in your journal, and finish this sentence: "Computers got small because…"

The answer is about 12 million times more. Let him read the big number out loud and count the zeros. If he's quick, ask: "ENIAC had 18,000 tubes in a room. How many rooms would 28 billion tubes need?" (Rough answer: over 1.5 million ENIAC rooms.)

5 · Exit ticket 5 min

Put these four in order, oldest first. Tap a card to move it.

What made computers small enough to fit in your pocket?

ENIAC used about 18,000 of which part?

Note his first-try score shown above, and whether the order puzzle was right first time, for your debrief with Claude.

6 · Homework mission ~30 min

  1. Time-traveller interview (15 min). Ask a grandparent or older relative:
    • What was the first computer, calculator or phone you ever used?
    • How big was it? What could it do?
    • What can your phone do now that would have seemed like magic then?
    Add their answer as a new stop on a timeline in your journal.
  2. Memory challenge (5 min). Close this page. Write down the 7 stops of today's story in order, from memory. Then open the page and check. Circle any you got wrong.
  3. Design a computer from 2050 (10 min). Draw it! Label: how big it is, what it's made of, and one thing it can do that computers can't do today.

🌌 Deep Space challenge (optional)

Find out what "Moore's Law" is. With your teacher, search the Computer History Museum website. Does the jump from 2,300 to 28 billion transistors fit it?

🔭 Explore more

Read alone = written for kids. Read together = written for grown-ups, so read it with your teacher. Watch = video.

  • The abacusRead alone · Britannica Kids

    Who invented the abacus, how it works with ones, tens and hundreds, and where people still use it today.

  • Science Trek: ComputersWatchRead alone · Idaho Public Television

    Short videos and facts for kids. What counts as a computer, how that changed over time, and is your phone a computer?

  • ComputerRead alone · Britannica Kids (Students)

    A longer article on how computers work and their history, including ENIAC. A good challenge read.

  • The Human Computer ProjectRead together · Smithsonian

    Before computers were machines, "computers" were people! Meet the six women who programmed ENIAC by setting switches and plugging cables. No manuals, no programming languages.

  • Babbage's Difference EnginesRead together · Science Museum, London

    The story of the machine that waited over 150 years to be built, and then worked perfectly.

  • Revolution: The First 2000 Years of ComputingRead together · Computer History Museum

    A huge online museum with photos of every machine from today's story. Great for browsing pictures.

Britannica Kids may show only part of an article without a school or library login, and access varies by country. The Smithsonian story is a nice set-up for 1.3. It also shows that "programming" began as a human job.
