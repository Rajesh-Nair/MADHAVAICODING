The AI Story: From "Can Machines Think?" to ChatGPT

Seventy-five years ago, a scientist asked a wild question: can a machine think? Today you can chat with one. This is the story of how we got here, and why the GPUs from last lesson turned out to be the secret ingredient.

⏱ 45 minutes✏️ Coder's Journal💻 Computer + an AI assistant (with your teacher)🏁 Last lesson of Chapter 1
Goal of the session: Madhav can tell the AI story in 4 eras. The key idea he should leave with: AI changed from following rules people wrote to learning from examples. That only took off when there was lots of data and GPUs to crunch it.
The win: he tries to write rules for "is it a cat?", finds why rules break, then catches (or doesn't catch!) an AI assistant on a planet fact.
Tone [PED-013]: optimistic but honest. Exciting tools, real mistakes, humans in charge.
Prep: have an AI assistant open on your account (Claude, ChatGPT or Gemini) and NASA Space Place in another tab. Answers are in teacher/0003-the-ai-story-key.md.
Scope note: this is the big-picture story. How an LLM predicts the next word gets its own hands-on lesson in Chapter 8. Don't go deep on transformers today.

2 · The AI story in four eras 14 min

Read one era at a time. Before each era, guess: what do you think machines could do back then?

Era 1 · The Dreamers (1950s)

  1. 💭1950

    Alan Turing asks: "Can machines think?"

    The British mathematician Alan Turing invented a game. A judge types questions to a hidden person and a hidden machine. If the judge can't tell which is which, maybe the machine is "thinking". We now call it the Turing test.

    Sources: Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy — Alan Turing · The Alan Turing Institute — Turing test

  2. 🏷️1955–1956

    The words "artificial intelligence" are born

    John McCarthy and three other scientists wrote a plan for a summer workshop at Dartmouth College. In it, they used a brand-new phrase: artificial intelligence. In summer 1956 they met to ask whether a machine could copy any part of human intelligence.

    Sources: CHM — The 1956 Dartmouth Workshop · Dartmouth — The Founding of AI

Era 2 · Machines that follow rules (1960s–1990s)

  1. 💬1966

    ELIZA, the first chatbot

    Joseph Weizenbaum at MIT made ELIZA. It followed simple rules: spot a word, flip it into a question. ("I feel sad" → "Why do you feel sad?") It understood nothing, but some people still felt it understood them!

    Source: CHM — Chatbots Decoded

  2. ♟️1997

    Deep Blue beats the world chess champion

    IBM's Deep Blue beat Garry Kasparov by checking about 200 million chess positions every second. Super-fast searching, following rules people programmed. It couldn't learn anything except chess.

    Source: IBM — Deep Blue

❄️ Honest bit: the "AI winters". Twice (in the 1970s, and again from the late 1980s), people promised AI would do amazing things soon. It didn't, so money and interest dried up. Lesson: be excited about AI, but check the claims!

Source: IBM — The History of Artificial Intelligence

Era 3 · Machines that learn from examples (1958 → 2016)

  1. 🧬1958

    The Perceptron: an idea ahead of its time

    Frank Rosenblatt built a machine loosely inspired by brain cells (neurons). Instead of following rules, it learned from examples. Great idea, but computers were far too slow, and there wasn't enough data. It had to wait over 50 years. (You'll build one yourself in Chapter 27!)

    Source: IBM — The History of AI

  2. 🖼️2012

    AlexNet: GPUs wake AI up

    A team in Toronto trained a neural network to recognise objects in photos. It learned from a million example images, which took trillions of maths operations, done on two NVIDIA gaming GPUs in a few days. It beat every other method in a huge picture contest by a big margin. Remember the GPU's thousands of helpers? This is exactly that kind of job!

    Sources: NVIDIA — Accelerating AI with GPUs · NVIDIA Developer — ImageNet challenge

  3. ⚫2016

    AlphaGo beats a Go champion

    Go is an ancient board game with more possible games than you could ever search. DeepMind's AlphaGo learned to play and beat champion Lee Sedol 4–1. Over 200 million people watched, and experts had thought this was a decade away.

    Source: Google DeepMind — AlphaGo

Era 4 · Machines that talk (2017 → today)

  1. 👀2017

    The Transformer: paying attention

    Google researchers published a paper called "Attention Is All You Need". Their new design, the Transformer, reads all the words in a sentence at once and works out which words matter most to each other. It was also great at using lots of GPUs together.

    Source: Google Research — Transformer

  2. 📚2018–2020

    GPT: bigger and bigger

    OpenAI built GPT models that learned by reading huge amounts of text. GPT-3 (2020) had 175 billion parameters. Think of them as tiny adjustable knobs it tuned while learning.

    Source: OpenAI — Language Models are Few-Shot Learners (GPT-3)

  3. 🗨️30 Nov 2022

    ChatGPT arrives

    OpenAI released ChatGPT, and suddenly anyone could chat with a large language model (LLM). Others followed, like Claude (Anthropic) and Gemini (Google).

    Source: OpenAI — Introducing ChatGPT

  4. 🚀Today

    The age of AI, and you're in it

    Today's AI assistants can explain homework, write code, and understand pictures. They're trained on enormous numbers of GPUs, the same "thousands of helpers" idea from Lesson 1.2, scaled up to whole buildings.

🔤 What does GPT stand for?

  • Generative: it creates new text.
  • Pre-trained: it learned by reading a huge amount of text before you ever talk to it.
  • Transformer: it uses the 2017 "paying attention" design.

⚖️ Optimistic but honest

  • 😃 AI is an amazing tool for learning, science and creating.
  • 🤔 It can make things up and sound very sure. (This is called a hallucination.) Always check important facts.
  • 🧑‍✈️ It learned from things humans wrote, so it can repeat human mistakes. People stay in charge.

That's why this course says: learn how it works, don't just use it.

Misconceptions to watch for:
(1) "AI was invented in 2022." The idea is over 70 years old. ChatGPT was just the moment everyone noticed.
(2) "AI thinks and knows things like a person." Today's chat assistants learned patterns in text. Treat them as clever tools that can be wrong. (Chapter 8 goes deeper.)
(3) "Deep Blue and ChatGPT are the same kind of AI." Deep Blue followed programmed rules plus searching. AlexNet, AlphaGo and GPT learned from examples.
Listen for: him connecting 2012 back to GPUs. That's the bridge between Lesson 1.2 and today.

3 · Unplugged: Rules vs Learning 10 min

Part A: be a rule-writing programmer (Era 2). Write rules a computer could follow to decide "Is this animal a cat?" For example: "IF it has whiskers AND pointy ears AND fur, THEN it's a cat."

Now your teacher will try to break your rules with tricky animals. Each time a rule breaks, fix it. How many rules do you need?

Part B: be a learning machine (Era 3). Your teacher shows (or describes) 6 animals and says "cat" or "not cat" for each. Then they name a new animal. Do you know the answer without any written rules?

Write in your journal: "Learning from examples is better than rules because…"

Trick animals that break typical rules: a tiger or lion (whiskers, pointy ears, fur, but not a house cat), a fox, a hairless (Sphynx) cat (breaks "has fur"), a cat with folded ears (breaks "pointy ears"), a cat statue. Let him patch the rules 3–4 times and feel the frustration. That frustration is the lesson: real-world things have too many exceptions for hand-written rules.
For Part B, use photos on your phone if you can. Learning from examples is how AlexNet worked, and later he'll train his own classifier (Capstone C).

4 · Mission: Fact-check an AI 8 min

With your teacher, on their account, ask an AI assistant:

How many moons does Mars have, and what are their names? Explain for an 11-year-old.

Then check the answer on NASA Space Place or NASA's Mars moons page.

Now ask it your own space or animal question, and fact-check that too.

Make a table: My question · What AI said · What NASA (or another trusted source) says · ✅ or ❌

Madhav types or dictates the prompt; you stay at the keyboard [AI-001]. It will very likely get the Mars question right. That's fine: the habit of checking is the skill, not catching a mistake.
If his own question is about something recent ("newest", "most moons", "latest discovery"), point out that AI can be out of date as well as wrong. Don't let him share personal information in prompts; the full privacy lesson is 8.2.

6 · Homework mission ~30 min

  1. AI spotter (10 min). Find 3 places AI is used in everyday life (maps, video recommendations, voice assistants, photo apps…). For each, write: what examples might it have learned from?
  2. Family AI survey (5 min). Ask a family member: "When did you first use an AI tool, and what for?" Add it to your Mission Log timeline.
  3. Memory challenge (7 min). Without looking, write the 4 eras of the AI story with one example each. Then check and circle anything you missed.
  4. Connect the lessons (5 min). In 2–3 sentences: why do AI companies need so many GPUs? Use the words "helpers" and "examples".
  5. My big question (3 min). Write one question about AI you want to be able to answer by the end of this course. Keep it safe. We'll come back to it!

🌌 Deep Space challenge (optional)

Play Turing's imitation game with your family. Two players hide in another room and answer written questions on paper. One of them tries to pretend to be the other! The judge reads the answers and guesses who is who. What questions were best at catching the pretender? Would those questions catch an AI?
