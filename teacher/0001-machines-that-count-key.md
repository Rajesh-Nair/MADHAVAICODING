## In-session answers

**Predict quiz:** ENIAC was about as heavy as twenty cars (30 tons ÷ about 1.5 t per car).

**Race sums**

| Sum | Answer | Carries |
|---|---|---|
| 34 + 25 | 59 | none |
| 47 + 38 | 85 | 1 (ones) |
| 126 + 275 | 401 | 2 (ones, tens) |
| 389 + 456 | 845 | 2 (ones, tens) |
| 999 + 1 | 1000 | 3 in a row, needs a 4th column (thousands) |

Sum 5 is the point of the race. He'll run out of columns. Ask: "What would Pascal's machine need?" (Another wheel.)

**Investigate:** 28,000,000,000 ÷ 2,300 ≈ **12 million times more** transistors (12,173,913).
Stretch: 28,000,000,000 ÷ 18,000 ≈ **1.56 million ENIAC rooms**.

**Journal sentence, "Computers got small because…":** a good answer mentions tiny switches (transistors) replacing big vacuum tubes, then lots of them on one chip.

**Exit ticket**
- Order: Pascaline → ENIAC → Transistor → Intel 4004
- Q1: Tiny transistor switches packed onto chips
- Q2: Vacuum tubes

## Homework: what good looks like

1. **Interview:** a real answer from a relative, added as a dated stop on his timeline. Bonus if he compares it with a lesson stop ("Grandpa's calculator came after the Intel 4004").
2. **Memory challenge:** the 7 stops are abacus → Pascaline (1642) → Babbage's Difference Engine (1820s–40s) → ENIAC (1945) → transistor (1947) → Intel 4004 (1971) → today's chips (billions of transistors).
   - Score out of 7. **5 or more is solid.**
   - Swapping ENIAC and the transistor is the most likely error. Note it for the debrief.
3. **2050 computer:** any imaginative design that has the 3 labels (size, material, new ability). Mark it for effort and labels, not realism.

**Scoring out of 10** (for the debrief):
- Interview: 3
- Memory challenge: 4 (round 7 → 4)
- 2050 drawing: 3

## Deep Space: Moore's Law
Moore's Law is the observation that the number of transistors on a chip roughly doubled about every two years.
2,300 (1971) → 28 billion (2024) is about 23–24 doublings in 53 years, so roughly every 2.2–2.3 years. That fits the trend well.
